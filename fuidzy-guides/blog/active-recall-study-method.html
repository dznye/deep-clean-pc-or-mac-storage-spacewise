Active recall study method: how to do it (with examples)

By the Fuidzy team · Updated 2026-10-09

Active recall means testing yourself instead of re-reading. Close the book, try to retrieve the answer, then check. The effort of retrieving is what makes the memory stick.

What active recall is

Active recall is the practice of pulling information out of your memory rather than putting it in again. Instead of re-reading a chapter, you close it and ask: what do I remember? Learning research consistently finds that retrieving information strengthens memory more than passive review, even when retrieval feels harder and less comfortable.

Why it feels worse but works better

Re-reading feels fluent, so you think you know the material. Retrieval exposes what you don't know, which feels like failing. That discomfort is the signal that learning is happening, and it tells you exactly where to focus next.

Five ways to do it

  1. Blank-page recall. Read a section, close the book, write down everything you remember, then check what you missed.
  2. Flashcards. One question per card, answered from memory before flipping.
  3. Practice questions. Past papers and problem sets are active recall in exam format.
  4. Teach it. Explain the topic aloud to an imaginary friend, a real one, or the wall.
  5. Question your notes. Turn headings into questions and answer them cold.

An example: biology

PassiveActive
Re-read the chapter on the Krebs cycleClose the chapter, draw the cycle from memory, then compare
Highlight key termsWrite each term's definition without looking, then check
Watch a lecture againPause after each section and summarise it aloud

How to fit it into a schedule

Active recall is most powerful when repeated over time. After first learning a topic, test yourself the same day, then a few days later, then a week later. A spaced repetition schedule turns that into a calendar. Short recall slots of ten minutes are easy to forget, so give each a time.

Common mistakes

  • Peeking at the answer too fast, which turns recall back into re-reading.
  • Only testing what you already know.
  • Making cards that are too big. One fact per card.
  • Never returning to the ones you got wrong.

Writing good questions

  • Ask for one fact or idea per question.
  • Prefer "why" and "how" over "what" when you want understanding.
  • Write questions in your own words, not copied from the text.
  • Add a question for anything that surprised you or that you got wrong.

A 20-minute routine

  1. 5 minutes: read or review the section once.
  2. 8 minutes: close the book and write everything you remember.
  3. 5 minutes: check against the source and mark the gaps.
  4. 2 minutes: write two questions about the gaps for next time.

Active recall versus other methods

MethodEffortEffect on long-term memory
Re-readingLowWeak, but feels productive
HighlightingLowWeak on its own
SummarisingMediumModerate, if done from memory

Common questions

What is active recall?

Studying by retrieving information from memory, such as quizzing yourself, instead of passively re-reading or highlighting.

Is active recall better than re-reading?

Research on learning generally finds retrieval practice produces stronger long-term memory than re-reading, though it feels harder.

How do I use active recall for notes?

Turn each heading into a question, hide the answers, and answer them from memory before checking.

How often should I practise active recall?

Little and often: a short session on the same day, then spaced out over days and weeks.

Do I need an app for active recall?

No. Pen and paper work. Apps help with scheduling and flashcards.
