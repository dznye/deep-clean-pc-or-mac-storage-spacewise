Organizing app for ADHD: keep it to one screen

By the Fuidzy team · Updated 2026-10-09

Organizing apps fail people when the system takes more effort than the thing being organized. Look for one screen, one-line capture, and nothing you have to categorise before you can start. This is general advice, not medical advice.

Why most organizing apps get abandoned

Many apps want you to build a system first: folders, tags, projects, areas. For anyone who struggles to start, the setup becomes the obstacle. The app that's still open in week three is usually the one that asked the least of you on day one.

General advice, not medical advice.

The low-friction checklist

  • One screen. Today, visible, without navigating.
  • One-line capture. Type "dentist, Thursday, 3pm" and it's placed.
  • No mandatory structure. You shouldn't have to decide whether something is a Project, an Area or a Resource.
  • Forgiving. Missed tasks should slide forward, not pile up in red.
  • Time is visible. See how much day is left, not only what is left to do.

A starter setup that takes five minutes

  1. Create nothing. Don't set up categories.
  2. Capture whatever is on your mind in one-line entries.
  3. Give today's three most important items a time.
  4. Let the rest sit in a single unsorted pile.
  5. Once a week, look at the pile and delete or schedule items.

Keep the system smaller than the work

A useful rule: if you spend longer maintaining the app than doing the tasks, it's too big. Start with a single list for today, add time to what matters, and ignore everything else until it earns a slot. Structure should grow out of use, not precede it.

Signs your system is too heavy

  • You open the app and feel tired before you've done anything.
  • You have more labels than tasks.
  • You re-organise the app instead of doing the work.
  • Items sit in 'inbox' for weeks.

A weekly reset routine

  1. Empty your pockets and bag of loose notes into the app (5 minutes).
  2. Look at next week's calendar and put times on the three things that matter.
  3. Delete or archive anything you won't do.
  4. Tidy the one place that stresses you most. One drawer, not the whole house.

Where do non-time things go?

Shopping lists, gift ideas, and 'someday' notes don't need a time. Keep them in one simple list, away from your day, and look at that list only when you're actually shopping or planning ahead. The day screen should stay for what's happening today.

Capture habits that stick

  • Use the same one-line format every time.
  • Capture first, sort never (or weekly).
  • If it takes more than ten seconds, you won't do it at 11pm.
  • Say it out loud or type it immediately; ideas disappear quickly.

What to put on your one screen

  • Today's three most important things, with times.
  • Anything fixed: appointments, meetings, pick-ups.
  • One habit you're building, placed on the schedule like any other block.
  • Nothing else. Everything else waits in the pile until it earns a slot.

A paper and digital hybrid

Some people think better with a pen. That's fine. A common compromise is to brain-dump on paper in the morning and put only the three or four items that matter into the app, with times. The paper holds the mess; the app holds the commitments.

Questions to ask when an app isn't sticking

  1. What's the first thing I stopped doing? Often it's capture or review; make that step easier.
  2. Is the app asking for decisions I don't want to make? Remove them or switch tools.
  3. Do I need it at all, or would a simple list and a calendar do?

Common questions

What is the best organizing app for ADHD?

The one you'll still use in week three: typically one screen, one-line capture and no required setup.

Do I need folders and tags?

Not to start. Structure should come after the habit of using the app, not before.

Is there a free organizing app?

Fuidzy is free, with a one-lane timeline and no install.

Why do I keep abandoning organizing apps?

Often the setup is heavier than the benefit. Start tiny, and add structure only when a real problem demands it.

Should I use paper instead?

If paper works for you, great. A digital app helps most when you want reminders and to move things easily.
