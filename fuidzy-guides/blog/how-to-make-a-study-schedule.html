How to make a study schedule that you'll actually follow

By the Fuidzy team · Updated 2026-10-09

A study schedule works when it's built from the hours you really have, not the hours you wish you had. Count your days, split the material into short sessions, schedule review, and leave a buffer. Then put it on a calendar you'll actually look at.

Step 1: count your real hours

Most schedules fail before they start because they assume time that doesn't exist. Before listing a single topic, work out your budget: days until the exam, minus days you can't study, minus a buffer day or two. Then decide how many focused hours you can truly manage per day. For most people that's far fewer than the hours they're awake.

Step 2: list what you need to cover

Write every subject and the topics inside it. Mark each topic as strong, okay or weak. Weak topics get more sessions and an earlier slot, because the early weeks are when you have time to recover from a bad start.

Step 3: split topics into sessions

A session should be small enough to finish and big enough to matter, usually 30 to 60 minutes on one narrow thing: "derivatives practice problems", not "maths". If a topic can't be described in a sentence, it's too big to schedule.

Step 4: add review on purpose

Learning something once isn't enough. Put short review sessions a few days after first learning a topic, and again a week later. They take ten minutes and do more for retention than another hour of re-reading. See the spaced repetition schedule for exact intervals.

Step 5: protect rest and leave a buffer

Schedule meals, sleep and a weekly day that's lighter than the others. Add one catch-up block per week for everything that slipped. A schedule with no slack is a schedule that breaks on Wednesday.

Making it on Google Calendar, Excel or Notion

Any tool works if you can see the week at a glance. In a calendar, create each session as an event with the topic in the title. In a spreadsheet, make columns for date, topic, minutes and done. In Notion, use a table with a date property. The weak point of all three is that moving a missed session means editing by hand, which is exactly when people give up.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with the topics you already like.
  • Scheduling every hour, so one slip breaks everything.
  • Colour-coding the plan for an hour instead of studying.
  • Never checking whether the plan matches what you actually did.

How many sessions does each subject get?

A simple way to allocate time: give each subject a score for exam weight (1 to 3) and a score for how weak you feel (1 to 3), multiply them, and share your sessions in proportion. A heavy exam you're weak in scores 9; a light one you're confident in scores 1. It stops you spending half your week on the subject you enjoy.

A finals schedule with three exams

Three exams across two weeks is a typical finals pattern. Work backwards from each date and give the earliest exam the earliest, densest slots, while keeping short review slots of the others so they don't go cold.

When the plan changes

Plans change: illness, a longer shift, a bad day. When it happens, don't rewrite everything. Move the missed session to your catch-up block, shorten the lowest-value topic, and carry on. A schedule is a living document, and the first sign it's working is how quickly you can adjust it.

Common questions

How do I make a study schedule for finals?

Count the days until each exam, list topics with your weakest first, split them into 30 to 60 minute sessions, add review slots and a weekly catch-up block.

How many hours should a study schedule include per day?

It depends on your load, but most people can manage only a few genuinely focused hours. Plan for focus, not for time at a desk.

What if I fall behind my study schedule?

Use your catch-up block, then cut or shorten the least valuable topics. Don't try to cram everything you missed.

Should I schedule breaks?

Yes. Schedule meals, sleep and short breaks like any other block.
